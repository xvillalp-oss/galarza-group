What really drives the cost of a build

Four decisions that move a budget more than any finish does, and why they are worth making before you sign.

Almost every budget conversation starts with finishes. They are the easiest part to picture: the marble, the joinery, the fixtures. But when you look at a finished project and compare it against the original estimate, the gap is rarely there.

It is earlier.

1. The land decides more than the design

A plot with good access, services at the curb and solid ground costs what it costs. One with difficult access, no water connection nearby, or poor fill starts with a bill nobody wrote down: hauling material, solving the connection, correcting the ground.

Before buying, it is worth asking what it takes to build there — not only what the square metre is worth.

2. Every change after construction documents is paid for twice

Moving a wall on the drawing costs an afternoon. Moving it once it is standing costs demolition, rebuilding, and adjusting everything that depended on it: services, plaster, floors, sometimes structure.

That is why construction documents are not paperwork. They are where the expensive decisions get made while they are still cheap.

3. Site supervision is not overhead

A project without its own supervision does not come out cheaper: it comes out slower, with more rework. Whatever is saved on the supervision line reappears, usually multiplied, in details that had to be done again.

4. Idle time costs money too

A permit that arrives late, a material ordered at the wrong moment, a subcontractor who did not show up. A stopped site keeps costing: equipment rental, security, crews waiting.

That is the real argument for keeping the whole cycle with one team. It is not about convenience: it is that nobody passes the blame while the clock runs.
